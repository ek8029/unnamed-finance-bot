A single Bloomberg Terminal costs $31,980 a year, which works out to $2,665 a month. Firms running more than one terminal pay $28,320 per seat per year, and every contract is a two-year lease.

Those are the two numbers people are looking for when they search this question. The rest of this post is about where they come from, what the lease actually looks like, what the money buys, why students sometimes get it for free, and why the price keeps going up rather than down.

## The price

Bloomberg does not publish a price list. Everything below comes from people who have seen the invoices.

The most specific recent source is [NeuGroup](https://connect.neugroup.com/public/blogs/bloomberg-terminals-how-much-more-youll-pay-next-year), a network of corporate treasurers, which reported on October 9, 2024 that Bloomberg was raising prices 6.5 percent for two-year subscriptions beginning or renewing on or after January 1, 2025. Their figures: $31,980 a year for a single terminal, and $2,360 a month, or $28,320 a year, for clients with more than one terminal. The multi-terminal rate had been $26,580 before the increase.

[Costbench](https://costbench.com/software/financial-data-terminals/bloomberg-terminal/), a pricing tracker, listed the same two figures with a "last verified" date of August 22, 2026, which is the most recent confirmation I could find.

Older sources give lower numbers, and some of them are still ranking on Google. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomberg_Terminal) cites Investopedia's July 2022 figure of around $24,000 per user, or $27,000 a year for subscribers with only one terminal. [Vendr's marketplace page](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/bloomberg) still quotes $24,000 to $27,000 per terminal for small firms, with volume pricing of $20,000 to $22,000 for large deployments. I read those as pre-2025 figures rather than a contradiction: the 2022 single-terminal price of $27,000 plus a couple of annual increases lands near the $31,980 NeuGroup reported.

| Configuration | Annual price | Source and date |
|---|---|---|
| Single terminal | $31,980 ($2,665 a month) | NeuGroup, Oct 2024, for 2025 renewals; Costbench re-verified Aug 2026 |
| Two or more terminals | $28,320 per terminal ($2,360 a month) | NeuGroup, Oct 2024 |
| Two or more terminals, pre-2025 | $26,580 per terminal | NeuGroup, Oct 2024 |
| Single terminal, 2022 | About $27,000 | Wikipedia citing Investopedia, July 2022 |
| Multi-terminal, 2022 | About $24,000 | Wikipedia citing Investopedia, July 2022 |

Two things are worth noticing in that table. First, the discount for buying more than one seat is about 11 percent, not the 30 or 40 percent that people sometimes assume. Second, the price has moved in one direction. The multi-terminal rate went from roughly $24,000 in 2022 to $28,320 in 2025, which is close to 18 percent in three years.

## What the lease looks like

Bloomberg does not sell the Terminal. It leases it, and according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomberg_Terminal) all terminals are leased in two-year cycles (three-year contracts were an option in the late 1990s and early 2000s). At $31,980 a year, a single seat is a $63,960 decision before the first login.

[Costbench](https://costbench.com/software/financial-data-terminals/bloomberg-terminal/) adds that the contract auto-renews unless terminated with 60 days notice. I could not verify that clause against a Bloomberg document, so it stands as a tracker's summary of what buyers report.

The list price is also not the whole bill. Wikipedia notes that many exchanges charge their own additional fees for real-time price feeds through the Terminal, and the same applies to some news organizations. The hardware is a separate question: Bloomberg's software runs on ordinary Windows PCs now, and most setups have between two and six displays, but the monitors are the firm's problem, not Bloomberg's.

### Bloomberg Anywhere versus a fixed seat

There are two ways a subscription can be set up. A fixed seat is tied to an installation on a specific machine, and [Bloomberg's access page](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/bloomberg-terminal/access) describes shared licenses that let multiple users log in to that one installation, which is how a university lab can run. Bloomberg Anywhere is tied to the person rather than the desk. Per [Bloomberg's B-Unit page](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/bloomberg-terminal/access/b-unit/), it "allows users to access their Bloomberg Terminal account from anywhere," authenticated by a fingerprint through either the free B-Unit mobile app or a credit-card-sized hardware B-Unit. Wikipedia describes Anywhere as the service that lets the Windows application connect over the internet, or through a Citrix web client, with companion apps for iOS and Android.

I could not find a published price difference between the two. Costbench lists Anywhere mobile access as included with the standard subscription. If there is a premium, it is negotiated, not posted.

## What the money buys

Here is what is inside the subscription, based on Bloomberg's own product pages and the university guides written for students learning the system.

**Data.** Real-time and historical pricing across equities, fixed income, currencies, commodities, and derivatives, from a company that describes itself on its [Terminal page](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/bloomberg-terminal/) as the "leading and largest provider of financial and business news." The fixed income side is where the depth is unusual. [BVAL](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/data/enterprise-catalog/pricing/evaluated-pricing), Bloomberg's evaluated pricing service, produces daily prices for more than 2.7 million securities, including bonds that rarely trade.

**News.** Bloomberg News is part of the subscription, and the Terminal page also describes a research team of more than 350 professionals behind the analytics.

**Instant Bloomberg.** The chat network. Bloomberg's own line is that "anyone who's anyone in financial services connects with clients, counterparties and colleagues on Instant Bloomberg." This is the part with no substitute, because the value is the list of people already on it.

**Functions.** The Terminal is operated by typing a short code and pressing GO. A few of the ones that show up in every training guide:

| Function | What it does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| FA | Financial statements (income, balance sheet, cash flow) with historicals and key ratios | University of San Diego guide |
| DES | Company snapshot: business overview, key metrics, segment data | University of San Diego guide |
| EQS | Screen the equity universe with custom filters | Yale guide |
| GP | Price chart with technical indicators and overlays | University of San Diego guide |
| PORT | Portfolio and risk analytics: attribution, exposures, scenario analysis | Bloomberg PORT brochure |
| BVAL | Evaluated prices for fixed income and derivatives | Bloomberg BVAL page |
| WEI | World equity indices dashboard | University of San Diego guide |

Sources for that table: the [University of San Diego](https://libguides.sandiego.edu/c.php?g=1305187&p=11445874) and [Yale](https://guides.library.yale.edu/Bloomberg/bloomberg_equites) library guides, and Bloomberg's [PORT brochure](https://data.bloomberglp.com/professional/sites/4/Portfolio_and_Risk_Analytics_Brochure4.pdf).

**Excel.** Per Wikipedia, the install ships with Excel add-ins that pull streaming, historical, and reference data into spreadsheets, and Bloomberg publishes free API SDKs in Python, Java, C++, and others for subscribers who want to build on top of the feed.

**Execution.** The Terminal page lists "integrated execution & order management solutions and sophisticated pre- and post-trade analytics." For a trading desk, the terminal is the order ticket as well as the research tool.

## What a Bloomberg Terminal actually does

The plainest description: it is one screen that answers almost any question about a security, a company, a market, or a counterparty, fast enough to act on, with a chat window to the person on the other side of the trade.

A credit analyst opens a bond, pulls the BVAL price, checks the issuer's financials in FA, reads the morning's Bloomberg News on the sector, and messages the dealer on IB to ask for a quote, all without leaving the application. A portfolio manager loads holdings into PORT and gets factor exposures and attribution against a benchmark. An equity analyst screens for companies with a set of ratios in EQS and drops the results into Excel with the add-in.

The unifying feature is not any single function. It is that the data, the news, the analytics, the messaging, and the execution share one login and one keyboard, and that the 325,000 to 350,000 other subscribers are on the same system. That is what a firm is paying $31,980 a seat for, and it is why the comparison to a $30 a month charting tool mostly misses the point.

## Students and universities

This is the one legitimate way to use a Bloomberg Terminal without paying for it.

[Bloomberg for Education](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/bloomberg-terminal/education/) places terminals in campus labs, and per an [EdTech Digest profile](https://www.edtechdigest.com/2025/09/10/bloomberg-for-education/) dated September 10, 2025, the program reached 1,100 universities and high schools worldwide. Bloomberg's [Terminal on Campus page](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/bloomberg-terminal/education/bloomberg-terminal-on-campus) says professors at more than 300 universities advise students in its Global Trading Challenge, and 8,400 students from 38 countries competed in 2023. The schools pay for the lab; students use it at no charge. Bloomberg does not publish what a university lab costs.

The certificate most students hear about is Bloomberg Market Concepts, or BMC. It is a self-paced e-learning course with four core modules (Economic Indicators, Currencies, Fixed Income, Equities), which [McGill's library guide](https://libraryguides.mcgill.ca/finance/BMC) puts at 8 to 12 hours. [Babson's guide](https://libguides.babson.edu/c.php?g=26345&p=7588385) describes a longer version with six core modules and two optional ones, about 12 hours total, so the module count depends on which edition the school is running.

What BMC costs depends entirely on where a student takes it:

- **At a campus terminal:** free. McGill's guide states that access "is free through the Bloomberg terminals," and Babson says the certificate is free for anyone who registers with a Babson email address.
- **Through a school sponsorship:** the [University of Tennessee's Haslam College](https://haslam.utk.edu/milc/market-concepts/) charges students $50 when BMC is taken as part of a finance course and $100 on its own, with the school covering the rest.
- **Online, with no school affiliation:** [WallStreetMojo](https://www.wallstreetmojo.com/bloomberg-market-concepts-bmc/) lists $149 for students and $249 for professionals, and a [July 2026 review](https://onlinecourseing.com/bloomberg-market-concepts-review/) quotes roughly the same, $149 and $249 for 12 months of access. I could not find a public price page on Bloomberg's own site, so these are third-party figures.

BMC is an introduction to markets, not a Terminal license. Finishing it does not grant access to a terminal. What it does is put a recognizable line on a resume for a few hours of work and, at a campus lab, zero dollars.

## Why the price does not fall

Every few years someone predicts that cheaper data will force Bloomberg's price down. The opposite keeps happening, and the reasons are structural.

**Increases are inflation-linked by policy.** NeuGroup's report quotes Bloomberg's explanation that price adjustments track "weighted global inflation over the prior two-year period." By that policy, the 6.5 percent increase for 2025 reflected the prior two years of inflation rather than a decision to test what the market would bear.

**The customer base is growing, not shrinking.** Whether the subscriber count is 325,000 or more than 350,000, the money in the industry around it is growing. Burton-Taylor's market sizing, reported by [Mondovisione](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/realtime-analytics-drive-record-growth-across-the-financial-market-data-landsca-2026326/) on March 26, 2026, put global spending on financial market data and analysis at $49.2 billion in 2025, up 6.5 percent, with Bloomberg, LSEG, and S&P Global as the three largest vendors.

**The network is the product.** A cheaper terminal with the same data would still lack the people on Instant Bloomberg. The chat network gets more valuable as it grows, and there is no way to buy into it except through the Terminal.

**There is no retail tier, on purpose.** The buyer is a firm with a compliance department and a data budget, and a single seat is a rounding error against the revenue that seat supports. None of Bloomberg's product pages list an individual plan, and the pricing structure (two-year lease, multi-seat discount, negotiated add-ons) is built around institutions.

For an individual, the practical conclusion is that Bloomberg's price is not a number to negotiate around. It is a number that describes a different customer.
